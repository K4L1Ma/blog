We keep looking for the villain.

When things go wrong, we want a saboteur: someone who meant it, who planned the outage, who traded the architecture for a bonus and a wink. We almost never find one. The people who sink our projects are pleasant, well-meaning, and completely certain they are not the problem. They refuse to think, and the refusal does the rest.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing from a Nazi prison cell, put it better than the rest of us: stupidity is a more dangerous enemy of the good than malice. Evil leaves a residue of unease, a conscience that can still be reached. Stupidity leaves nothing. It cannot be argued with or shamed out of a position it never reasoned itself into. Carlo Cipolla, a Berkeley economist, gave the disease a sharper edge. His third law defines the type: a stupid person causes losses to others while gaining nothing, sometimes losing in the bargain. The bandit is selfish and predictable. The stupid take no cut and burn the field.

The force at work here is not ignorance. Ignorance is missing information, and a document and an afternoon will cure it. Stupidity is having the information and the tools and the time, then choosing the autopilot anyway. The dumbest person in the room who asks why is saner than the most credentialed one who does not.

> The bandit takes a cut. The stupid one burns the field and calls it progress.

Add AI to this and the refusal stops being a personal flaw. It becomes infrastructure. When two people each delegate their judgment and neither questions the output, they form what I have started calling the stupidity spiral. The producer ships what the model wrote without reading it. The reviewer approves it by asking the model to check its own work. The checksum passes the checksum. The pull request merges with three hundred and sixty files changed and no human who can explain a single one of them. Nobody conspired. Two people politely decided not to think, and it was remarkably fast.

A team of engineers once outlasted everyone in their company. Eight years, through reorgs and layoffs, they alone remained. Not because they were the best, but because they were the most durable against stupidity: they absorbed every bad decision with a docile agreement and matched every act of nonsense with more stupidity. The organization read the survival as competence. They were promoted as the proof and handed the new hires, who learned the reflex quickly. Never challenge the status quo. Do as you are told. Let the tool decide.

Before an incident that arrived three weeks later, a fix was proposed with its consequences spelled out. They called it overengineered. They preferred the version that required no thought. When the incident landed exactly as described, they proposed the same fix back as a fresh idea. Cipolla called stupidity a constant fraction of any population. The people are not evil. They are everywhere, in every seat, making decisions they have never agreed to understand.

So the remedy is not better people. It is less surrender. We built an industry that lets engineers stop thinking and calls it velocity. The vendors take a cut of the surrender, and like the cigarette seller they profit from the harm they cause. But the match is ours to strike. Delegating the thinking is a choice we make again and again, not a thing done to us, and it holds at every scale: a team, a company, a country, a world of people quietly on autopilot.

Cheaper code generation will keep arriving, and so will the incident three weeks after the warning. We cannot prompt our way out of what nobody understands. The teams that endure will be the ones that ask why before they merge.
